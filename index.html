Unveiling technology · the heart-rate sensor in a smartwatch

Your watch takes your pulse with light.

A green LED on the back of the watch shines into your wrist. With every heartbeat a little more blood squeezes into the vessels under the skin, and blood swallows green light. A sensor next to the LED sees the light dim and recover about once a second, and the watch counts the dips.

Try this (about two minutes)

  1. Watch the circles. Each one is a beat the watch counted; the red marks underneath are the real heartbeats.
  2. Press “Light in skin”. Watch green light get swallowed by blood. When there’s more blood, less light gets back to the sensor: that dip is your heartbeat.
  3. Drag “Arm movement” past halfway as if you were running. The reading drifts away from your real pulse.
  4. Set the movement rhythm to 120 steps/min. The watch now counts your steps as heartbeats.
  5. Tick “Use the accelerometer”. The watch learns what your arm's motion looks like and subtracts it. Your heart comes back.
  6. Switch the LED to red with some movement on. Now you know why watches glow green.
  7. Try your own pulse with the camera button at the top.

The watch never feels your pulse. It watches your skin change colour, and a swinging arm changes the colour too.

Why green?

Blood's haemoglobin absorbs green light about 125 times more strongly than red, and about 33 times more than infrared. Green also stops in the top millimetre of skin, which is packed with tiny arteries and far from the tendons and veins that slosh when you move. Is it safe? Yes: the LEDs are ordinary visible light at a few milliwatts, flashed briefly. No UV, no noticeable heat.

Turn up skin tone: melanin also absorbs green strongly, so less light comes back and sensor noise matters more. Real watches compensate by driving the LED harder, which costs battery. This is a known fairness problem for optical sensors. A phone camera uses red because the light must travel through your whole fingertip, and only red gets that far.

How the watch tells a beat from noise

  1. Linearise. Light falls off exponentially with the amount of blood (the Beer–Lambert law), so the code takes −log(light). Now a 1% dip means 1% more absorption whatever the brightness: that's the – perfusion index.
  2. Band-pass filter 0.5–3.5 Hz (30–210 BPM). Slower changes (breathing, pressing harder) and faster ones (sensor hiss) can't be a heartbeat, so two cascaded filters remove them.
  3. Peak picking. A point counts as a beat if it is the highest within ±0.25 s, rises above half the biggest recent swing, and comes after a short refractory gap, so each beat's smaller echo wave is not counted twice.
  4. Spectrum check. A Fourier transform of the last 8 s finds the strongest rhythm. If counting and spectrum disagree, the reading is unreliable.
  5. Motion cancelling. An adaptive filter (normalised LMS, 24 taps) predicts the light wiggle from the accelerometer, sample by sample, and subtracts it. It can't help when your steps exactly match your heartbeat: then motion and pulse look identical.

What this simulation leaves out

  • The wrist model uses order-of-magnitude constants, not measured tissue optics; real absorption depends on wavelength in a more complicated way.
  • Real watches use several LEDs and photodiodes, change LED brightness automatically, and run trained models on top of filtering like this.
  • Sampling at 30 Hz limits timing to about ±17 ms per beat; above ~180 BPM the beat count starts to drift low.
  • Blood-oxygen (SpO₂) uses the same sensor with red and infrared together. That's a different calculation, not shown here.
